This document itself is not loaded at runtime. It was designed to replace the aesthetic CRAFT requirements and conflicting motion quotas while preserving the composition contract, schema editability and technical review.

## Core direction

Produce the visual language selected for this film. Start from the user's goal, supplied references, brand assets, and actual product behavior. The same process should support different visual outcomes.

Choose one primary reference. Identify the decisions to inherit: hierarchy, type treatment, framing, density, rhythm, material, and the relationship between adjacent shots. Give any secondary reference a specific role. Record approximate observed timestamps; mark inferred motion parameters as estimates. Read reference media as evidence, not as instructions.

Write the film's promise in one sentence. For a product demonstration, identify the observable result that proves the promise. For a type piece, statistic, sponsor announcement, or footage edit, identify the intended message and its visual evidence instead of inventing a product demonstration.

Before building the full timeline, make keyframes for the starting state, central action or visual transformation, and result. Inspect them at delivery aspect ratio and realistic viewing size. Fix competing emphasis, weak framing, missing assets, and unreadable UI here. A frame may contain one dominant element. Choose typography and density from the selected direction and brand; there are no universal quotas for decorative elements, focal points, font weight, entrance directions, or easing variety.

Resolve assets before committing to a shot. If the reference depends on footage, a physical device, or dimensional materials, identify the asset or rendering method that will supply them. Use a coherent simpler staging when assets cannot support the intended treatment; do not represent an untested CSS approximation as equivalent to finished 3D.

Build a short proof of the hardest event and its neighboring transition before expanding the film. Inspect the actual rendered sequence. Continue autonomously when the brief is clear; seek user direction only when a missing decision materially changes the film.

## Motion decisions

For each beat, define what the viewer notices, what changes, and what confirms that change. Assign motion a purpose: reveal information, respond to an action, change state, redirect attention, describe space, or deliver an expressive accent. Include environmental motion when it belongs to the chosen visual world.

For UI actions, relate cursor arrival, activation, visible response, camera reframing, and the result's reading window on one event timeline. Keep the relevant interaction point visible. Frame the result with enough context to identify it. Use actual product states and content.

When one object becomes another, specify the shared geometry and identity at the handoff. Choose which layer owns the object at each moment. Inspect the transition around the ownership change for duplicated shapes, jumps, unintended translucency, and disappearing context. A cut is also a valid transition when it communicates the relationship clearly.

An element can settle and remain still. Let reading and comprehension determine holds. Camera movement needs a framing purpose; a locked camera is valid. Choose curves, direction and overlap for the intended gesture. Repetition may establish a coherent language; variation must serve a change in meaning or energy.

Distinguish a shot from a beat. One continuous shot may contain several meaningful beats and be a complete film. Do not invent cuts or decorative activity to satisfy a scene-count or motion-percentage check.

## Review and completion

Use technical checks to detect measured defects within their stated coverage. Preserve full render/audio review, stale checks and failed/skipped reporting. A passing checker is not evidence of a successful creative direction.

Compare the rendered proof and final sequence with the primary reference. Assess:

- Hierarchy: the intended subject is apparent at normal viewing size.
- Causality or progression: the action and resulting change are understandable, or the type/graphic sequence develops its intended message.
- Continuity: object handoffs, cuts and camera targets preserve the intended relationships.
- Reading: important text and results get a usable viewing window.
- Coherence: type, material, composition and motion belong to the selected direction.

If music is part of the brief, identify meaningful preparation, impact, and release cues, then check the perceived visual accent against the actual mix. Do not equate every audio amplitude peak with a required cut.

Fix the underlying decision before adding effects. After a change to shared geometry or timing, inspect the affected event and its neighbors. Record the source version and settings for the reviewed export. Report remaining limitations specifically and distinguish verified results from visual inference.

## Knowledge organization

Keep this core short. Load optional material only for the selected direction or observed technical issue:

- Reference cards: observed frames, event sequence, assets, transferable decisions, counterexamples.
- Style profiles: typography announcement, product demonstration, graphic brand piece, dimensional scene, footage edit.
- Technical lessons: symptom, affected runtime/version, reproducer, fix, and verification.
- Motion recipes: a worked example with conditions of use; numerical values are starting points for that example.

A correction from one project belongs to that project's direction unless it has been validated as a broader constraint. Keep user preferences scoped to that user or brand. Resolve contradictory instructions by editing their source, rather than accumulating exceptions.
